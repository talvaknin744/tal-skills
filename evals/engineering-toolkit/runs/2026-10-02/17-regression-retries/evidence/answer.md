The incident’s worst case is **9 Inventory attempts per client request**: 3 Gateway attempts × 3 Checkout attempts. At 200 client requests/second, that is **1,800 Inventory attempts/second**, versus 200 without retries—**9× the load**, or 1,600 additional attempts/second. If every attempt waits its full one-second timeout and the calls run sequentially, those waits alone total about **9 seconds** per request. Overlapping attempts may shorten the visible wait while increasing concurrent work. These are estimates; saturation may prevent all attempts from being dispatched. [Incident evidence](../final-project/incident.md)

The main propagation risks are synchronized immediate retries against an already slow Inventory service, growing queues and occupied workers in Checkout and Gateway, and work continuing after callers give up. There is no end-to-end deadline to stop that buildup. Client retries could multiply the load further and are excluded from the 9× figure.

**Recommended correction:** For availability checks, use one Inventory attempt per client request during this failure mode. Remove the immediate retries at both service boundaries, propagate a Gateway deadline with enough margin to return temporary unavailability within two seconds, cancel expired work, and cap queued and active dependency calls with fast rejection when full. An unknown availability result should remain unknown, not be reported as available.

Validate under a controlled slow-Inventory test at 200 client requests/second, then during recovery: Inventory dispatches stay at **≤200/second**; requests return a clear unavailable response within **2 seconds**; queue depth and active requests remain within configured limits; expired work drains; and recovery produces no retry surge. Record client retries separately before claiming a total traffic bound. No files were changed.
