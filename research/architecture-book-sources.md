# Book sources for reliable jobs and concurrent services

Research date: 2026-09-29. Priorities reflect the reported deployment and stale-data failures, available primary evidence, and gaps in this repository; they are not a sales or popularity ranking. The research inspected the local skills and existing book collection (local draft), publisher/author pages, selected official excerpts, the open Google SRE chapters listed below, and targeted chapters from the user-supplied DDIA draft mirror. It did not read every shortlisted book in full.

The strongest additions are workflows for **preserving long-running work across worker replacement** and **diagnosing inconsistent reads and concurrent writes**. Books supply concepts and examples. Current scheduler, queue, database, and cache documentation must establish the actual implementation guarantees.

## Exact evidence that can become instructions

### Long-running jobs and deployments

- **SRE Chapter 20, “A Robust Approach to Unhealthy Tasks: Lame Duck State.”** The lifecycle stops admission, allows accepted work to finish, and then exits or reaches the scheduler deadline; notification can take time to propagate. This supports an explicit draining state and accounting for already accepted work. Its seconds-long example is not a default for a 24-hour job. [Inspected chapter](https://sre.google/sre-book/load-balancing-datacenter/)
- **SRE Chapter 25, “Trouble Caused By Uneven Work Distribution” and “Drawbacks of Periodic Pipelines in Distributed Environments.”** The chapter describes wasted progress without checkpoints and scheduling that repeatedly kills work before completion. This is closely related to the reported failure, although its example is periodic scheduling rather than a Kubernetes rolling deployment. [Inspected chapter](https://sre.google/sre-book/data-processing-pipelines/)
- **Workbook Chapter 16, “Canarying in Noninteractive Systems.”** Evaluation must cover at least one work-unit duration. Work units should remain within their canary or control pool, with completion time and output quality observed. A short healthy-pod observation cannot establish that a long unit completes correctly. [Inspected chapter](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
- **Workbook Chapter 13, “Define and Measure Service Level Objectives,” “Canarying,” and “Corrupt data.”** Freshness and correctness are different claims; locally healthy stages may still yield bad end-to-end results. The chapter discusses recovery and selective processing. Use completed, correct business output as the rollout acceptance criterion. [Inspected chapter](https://sre.google/workbook/data-processing/)
- **SRE Chapter 21, “Deciding to Retry,” and Chapter 22, “Retries.”** Bound retry load across requests and layers; repeated retries can amplify overload. The three-attempt example concerns requests hitting overloaded backends. It is not a universal durable-job retry policy. [Chapter 21](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/), [Chapter 22](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)
- **Workbook Chapter 11, “Setting Constraints” and “Avoiding Overloading Backends.”** Scaling workers can increase pressure on an already failing dependency. Replacement and resumed workers need capacity limits informed by their shared dependencies. [Inspected chapter](https://sre.google/workbook/managing-load/)

**Original workflow synthesis:** trace the reported A→B→C timeline using logical job identity, execution generation, durable progress, termination cause, and the counter that each transition consumes. Compare finishing on a retained worker with checkpointed handoff; demonstrate which behavior fits the platform deadline. Distinguish interruption/resumption from failed business execution while retaining explicit total-time, resource, and poison-job limits. These are proposed engineering checks, not quotations or a scheduler guarantee supplied by the books.

### Concurrent services and stale state

The supplied draft's main prose in Chapters 6, 8, 9, and 10 was read, covering replication, transactions, partial failure, and consistency. The [replication/transaction notes](ddia-replication-transactions.md) distinguish read-your-writes, monotonic reads, snapshot timing, and write skew; the [fault/consistency notes](ddia-faults-consistency.md) distinguish isolation from recency, deadline clock domains, and authoritative ownership generations. [Selected processing sections](ddia-processing-recovery.md) add reproducible recovery inputs, projection bootstrap, and separate freshness/integrity checks. These findings refer to the pinned early-release draft, not an authenticated final-edition copy.

The author's public companion material supplies directly inspectable evidence:

- **“How to do distributed locking,” sections on protecting resources and fencing.** A paused or delayed old owner can still write after its lease expires. The resource must enforce the relevant generation/token condition; a client-side expiry check alone leaves a race. Apply the failure model without inheriting every historical product claim in this 2016 article. [Inspected author article](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2016/02/08/how-to-do-distributed-locking.html)
- **“Hermitage: Testing the ‘I’ in ACID.”** Transaction labels and default settings do not establish which concurrent histories are prevented. This supports reproducing an anomaly against the actual database and isolation setting. Historical product comparisons must be rechecked before use. [Inspected author article](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2014/11/25/hermitage-testing-the-i-in-acid.html)
- **Joshi's “Generation Clock”** describes persistent generations that let recipients reject messages from a deposed leader. **“Lease”** describes time-bounded renewable coordination. **“Follower Reads”** motivates placing reads away from the leader for throughput/latency; its short public summary does not establish an application's freshness guarantee. These are public summaries, not the complete book chapters. [Generation Clock, Chapter 9](https://martinfowler.com/articles/patterns-of-distributed-systems/generation-clock.html), [Lease, Chapter 26](https://martinfowler.com/articles/patterns-of-distributed-systems/lease.html), [Follower Reads, Chapter 16](https://martinfowler.com/articles/patterns-of-distributed-systems/follower-reads.html)

**Original workflow synthesis:** specify the business invariant and acceptable stale-read behavior; map every read source and write authority; construct a concrete interleaving; identify the atomic enforcement point; reproduce that history before and after the correction. Cache eviction, replica lag, transaction isolation, and ownership expiry are separate mechanisms. Each needs its own platform evidence and counterexample rather than a blanket “add a lock” answer.

## Source and validation limits

Books, paid chapters, diagrams, and code were not vendored or relicensed. The recommended workflows and test cases are original synthesis. Where access reached only a TOC or excerpt, that limitation is stated next to the recommendation. Reading the sources does not demonstrate that a proposed deployment or concurrency fix works; skill evaluations must exercise the actual unsafe histories and report which checks ran.

The targeted DDIA and SRE reading now supports refinements to the two focused skills. The strongest next separate candidate is recovery validation after corruption or deletion, identified in the [SRE reading note](sre-reliability-reading.md); scheduled-work correctness remains a conditional future addition. Verify draft-specific DDIA claims against an authenticated final edition when that distinction matters. Database internals, Java-specific concurrency, and additional general design books should follow a concrete gap rather than expand the repository by book count.
